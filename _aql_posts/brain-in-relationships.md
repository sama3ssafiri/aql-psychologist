A late reply. A change in someone’s tone. An argument with someone you love. Small moments can bring up big feelings.

This page helps you explore those feelings and think about what you might do next. Select a scenario in the brain model, then try the practice idea beside it.

## What does the brain research tell us?

**Feeling rejected can be upsetting in a very real way.** Studies have found changes in brain activity when people remember rejection or feel left out. In one study, people who worried more about their close relationships showed stronger responses in some areas during a game where they were left out.

**Support from someone we trust may help.** In a small study, 16 married women waited for a possible electric shock. Holding their husband’s hand reduced some brain responses linked with the threat. Some responses were also lower in women who felt better about their marriage. That does not mean touch helps everyone, or that reassurance always solves a worry.

These findings help us understand why relationships can affect us strongly. They do not tell us that a fear is true, that our brain is broken, or which treatment one person needs. Each brain area has many jobs. There is no single “jealousy spot” or “abandonment spot”.

## So what can I do with this?

A useful place to start is the pattern around a difficult moment:

**What happened → what I thought it meant → what I felt → what I did → what happened next**

For example: “They haven’t replied → they’re leaving me → fear and a tight chest → I send more messages → I feel better briefly, then start checking again.”

That is one possible pattern, not an explanation for every relationship problem. Sometimes another person really is unreliable or hurtful. That needs attention too.

With your psychologist, you can work out which part of your own pattern might be helpful to change.

| If you notice… | A small step to explore together | A sign it might be helping |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You quickly assume the worst | Separate what you know from what you are guessing. Consider another possible explanation. | You can pause before acting on the first explanation. |
| You keep checking for reassurance | Agree on a clear way to ask for support. Try a small, planned pause before repeating the same question. | You can return to an activity without checking as often. |
| You feel overwhelmed during a conversation | Practise settling yourself, asking for a break and agreeing when to return. | You can come back and say what you need more clearly. |
| You pull away instead of asking for help | Try one clear request with someone you trust. | You can ask for support and notice how they respond. |

These are examples to discuss, not a treatment plan everyone should follow. A pause should help you choose what to do; it should not mean putting up with harm or ignoring a real need.

## What could a plan look like?

Here is an example for someone who finds waiting for replies difficult:

1. **Choose a goal that matters.** “I want to stay involved in my evening instead of checking my phone throughout it.”
2. **Understand what happens.** Notice the situation, the worry, the urge to check and whether checking helps beyond the next few moments.
3. **Try a small step.** With your psychologist, choose a manageable wait before checking again during an ordinary, non-urgent situation. Decide what you will do during that time.
4. **Compare your prediction with what happened.** What did you expect? What actually happened? What helped you cope, even if you still felt worried?
5. **Review and adjust.** Keep, change or replace the step based on what you learned and what is happening in the relationship.

This kind of practice is sometimes called a **behavioural experiment**: a small, planned way to test a worry in everyday life. Research supports this approach for some anxiety problems. The example above is an adaptation to discuss with your psychologist; the brain studies did not test this phone-checking plan.

Progress might mean fewer repeated checks, returning to your usual activities, asking for help more clearly, or handling a disagreement without losing the rest of your day. You do not need to feel calm all the time to make progress.

## What is the brain model showing?

The white outlines point to areas discussed in the studies. They are rough guides, not a scan of your brain. The glow and moving lights are visual effects, not a measure of how strongly you feel something.

Some everyday examples, such as waiting for a reply, use research about related situations. Those exact examples were not tested in the brain studies. Your psychologist will build a plan around your needs, history, relationships and goals—not around which area lights up here.

## Read the research (optional)

- [Rejection research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3761131/): a review combining results from several brain-imaging studies. The model shows selected areas reported when people remembered rejection by someone important to them.
- [Relationship worries and being left out](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21467049/): a study of attachment anxiety during a social-exclusion game. It did not test unanswered messages or fear of abandonment in everyday relationships.
- [Supportive hand-holding](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17201784/): a small study involving married women facing a possible electric shock. It did not test every kind of reassurance or secure relationship.
- [Remembering jealousy](https://iris.unimore.it/handle/11380/1190085): a study involving women who had experienced infidelity. Its findings do not describe every experience of jealousy.
- [Learning to cope with uncertainty](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36229113/): a treatment study in adults with generalised anxiety disorder. People practised testing worries about uncertainty. It supports the practice approach, not a particular relationship plan or brain-area treatment.
